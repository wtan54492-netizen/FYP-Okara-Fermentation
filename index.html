Abstract

Okara, the pulp left after making soy milk or tofu, contains about 25% protein, 50% dietary fibre and 10% fat on a dry-matter basis, although its composition varies (Spiess et al., 2025). Fresh okara contains a lot of water, which makes it difficult to handle and store. In this project, I tested how reducing the starting moisture affected visible fungal growth and crude protein after 48 hours of fermentation with Rhizopus oligosporus. The pilot trial covered 50%, 55%, 60%, 65% and 72.5% initial moisture on a wet basis. The two driest samples showed less visible white growth. At the 72.5% starting moisture target, estimated crude protein was 25.7% of dry mass for the unfermented baseline and 36.7% for the fermented sample, using target moisture for the conversion. The difference in appearance between 55% and 60% gives me a starting point for the next trial.

From mycelium protein to a Singapore substrate

I first learnt about mycelium protein and how it is produced. Filamentous fungi grow as branching threads called hyphae, and the network they form is called mycelium. One commercial example is mycoprotein made from Fusarium venenatum (United States Pharmacopeia, 2022). This made me wonder whether I could use a food by-product from Singapore to grow fungi. I focused on solid-state fermentation (SSF) because the fungus can grow directly on a moist solid. In this study I analyse the whole fermented product, so the crude protein result includes both the okara and the fungal material.

I then looked at food waste in Singapore, especially by-products that could be collected separately from food manufacturers. The National Environment Agency reported that Singapore generated 790,000 tonnes of food waste in 2025 and recycled 18%. Enterprise Singapore listed okara, brewer’s spent grain, surplus bread, and fruit and vegetables among the larger food manufacturing side streams. I focused on okara, spent grain and bakery surplus. I compared how easily I could get each material, how much it might vary, how I would prepare it, and whether earlier studies had shown that fungi could grow on it (National Environment Agency, n.d. and Enterprise Singapore, 2023).

I considered surplus bread because Sandoval et al. (2024) had already fermented it with Rhizopus oligosporus. However, if I collected different types of bread, the recipes could vary in sugar, fat, salt and preservatives. The bread could also start with different amounts of water. To control these factors, I would need to collect the same type of bread or check the composition of each batch. Brewer’s spent grain was another option. The NUS Myco-genesis project treated it with dilute acid before fermenting it with Neurospora crassa, which gave me an example of the extra preparation that this route could involve (NUS EDIC, n.d.). I chose okara because it comes from a specific production process, retains protein and fibre, and can be used as a solid substrate. NUS studies had also used fermented okara in drinks and biscuits (National University of Singapore, 2018 and Lee et al., 2023).

Compared with a mixture of bakery products, okara gave me a more clearly defined starting material. This made it easier to keep the type of substrate consistent and focus on one experimental variable at a time. Once I found a supplier, using okara from one source also helped limit variation in the material during the pilot. I then looked at the practical problems with handling okara to decide which variable to study first.

Choice of fungus and process variable

After choosing okara, I looked at Rhizopus oligosporus and Neurospora intermedia. Maini Rekdal et al. (2024) showed that N. intermedia can grow on okara in Indonesian red oncom. I chose R. oligosporus because it is used in soy tempeh and I could get a commercial tempeh starter for the pilot. Vong et al. (2018) had also shown that R. oligosporus could ferment okara on its own. Using the same starter across the treatments let me focus on changing the starting moisture.

Vong et al. (2018) also tested R. oligosporus together with the yeast Yarrowia lipolytica and reported changes in fibre and free amino acids. This made me interested in the nutritional changes during fermentation. For my pilot, I used Dumas analysis, which estimates crude protein from the total nitrogen in the sample. I would need separate tests to measure protein quality, the amino-acid composition or how much fungal biomass was produced.

After speaking with two companies, I looked more closely at how okara is handled before fermentation. Pawsible Foods mentioned storage, contamination and the difficulty of controlling the composition of the starting material, including the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio. Moonbeam found soy okara fairly consistent across its suppliers, but said that wet pulp was difficult to handle and could spoil. Helstad et al. (2023) also described fresh okara as highly perishable and reported that its composition can vary by source. These discussions helped me choose an issue that I could study in a small lab experiment.

I started thinking about whether removing some water before fermentation could make the pulp easier to handle. Removing water means less weight to handle for the same amount of okara solids. However, the drier material still has to support fungal growth. Too little available water could limit growth, while very wet, densely packed pulp could make it harder for air to reach the fungus (Sitanggang et al., 2020). I could adjust and measure moisture with the equipment I had, so I decided to test how much I could reduce it and how that affected both visible fungal growth and crude protein after 48 hours. I would need separate experiments to find out whether a lower starting moisture also improves shelf life or reduces contamination.

I also considered comparing suppliers, testing different drying methods and changing the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio. Different suppliers could give me samples that varied in moisture, composition and how they had been handled before collection. Different drying methods might also change the structure of the pulp. Changing the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio could mean adding an ingredient or doing extra tests to find out what was already in the pulp. I started with moisture because I could adjust it directly and use the pilot to decide which conditions to test again.

The industry problem involves both the amount of water producers handle and the consistency of the material they receive. Even if removing water reduces the wet mass, the practical value depends on what happens in fermentation. If a lower moisture level still supports visible growth and gives comparable crude protein, it becomes a condition worth testing again. If growth drops off, that tells me where removing more water may stop being useful for this process.

Research gap, hypothesis and scope

My main question was how lowering the starting moisture of fresh okara affects visible mycelial growth and crude protein after 48 hours with the R. oligosporus starter. Based on the pilot photos, I expect samples starting at 50–55% moisture to show less surface growth than samples starting at 60–72.5% when I repeat them under the same conditions. I also want to see how crude protein concentration compares across the moisture levels on a dry-matter basis.

Previous studies have used R. oligosporus to ferment okara. Vong et al. (2018) studied nutrition and flavour, while Sitanggang et al. (2020) looked at water activity, antioxidant activity and protein breakdown. Koptelova et al. (2024) tested ways of reducing moisture and selected 70% for their main nutritional comparison. Phan Nhat Sam (2026) examined moisture in relation to cake formation.

The studies I reviewed leave an open question about how the initial moisture content affects the nutritional composition of okara after fermentation. Koptelova et al. (2024), for example, explored different ways to adjust moisture, then focused their nutritional comparison on the product made at the selected moisture level. In my pilot, I compare estimated dry-basis crude protein across different initial moisture levels after 48 hours of fermentation with R. oligosporus. I also use photos to see whether differences in visible fungal growth follow the same pattern as the protein estimates.

The pilot helps me decide which moisture levels to repeat, particularly around 55–60%, where the photos showed a difference in surface growth. I plan to prepare these levels side by side and compare surface coverage and crude protein again. The comparison is exploratory because the pilot has one protein result per fermented moisture level and the dry-basis values were estimated using the starting moisture targets.

I express starting moisture on a wet basis because I can estimate it from the mass lost during oven drying. This gives me a practical way to define and compare the moisture levels using the oven and balance available for the pilot.

Pilot methods and moisture selection

I estimated the starting moisture of the reference batch using five portions of fresh okara. I weighed each portion before drying, then dried and reweighed it until its mass stopped changing. For each portion, I subtracted the final dry mass from the initial wet mass, divided by the initial wet mass and multiplied by 100. The average of the five wet-basis moisture values was 72.5%. I used that average as the highest starting moisture level in the pilot. I fermented the 72.5% treatment like the other treatments and submitted a separate unfermented sample at the same target as the baseline.

The pilot trial included five starting moisture levels. I used 72.5% as the reference, with 65% and 60% to test moderate reductions and 55% and 50% to see what happened when more water was removed. Below 65%, the targets are spaced five percentage points apart. The full range covers 22.5 percentage points. At each level, I looked at visible fungal growth and crude protein content.

If 100 g of okara starts at 72.5% moisture, it would weigh 68.75 g at 60% and 55 g at 50%, assuming the dry mass stays the same. This calculation helps me plan how much water to remove.

Heat treatment and cooling

I heat-treated the okara in a pressure cooker for about 30 minutes, based on my recollection of the pilot procedure. I let the okara cool before inoculation without using a separate cooling procedure.

Moisture adjustment

I calculated the target wet mass by dividing the estimated dry mass of okara by the target dry-matter fraction (1 minus the target moisture fraction). I used a forced-air oven to remove water and added distilled water when needed to bring the okara to the calculated target mass. I mixed the okara well after each adjustment to distribute the moisture.

Inoculation and incubation

I used Craft & Culture’s commercial tempeh starter (ragi), which the supplier identified as Rhizopus oligosporus (Craft & Culture, n.d.). I mixed 1 g of the commercial starter into the okara for every 100 g of okara dry matter. I incubated the samples for 48 hours at an incubator setpoint of 30 °C. Koptelova et al. (2024) provide a published basis for using 48 hours in okara fermentation with R. oligosporus. In their setup, growth was incomplete at 24 hours and the product spoiled by 72 hours. I used 48 hours as a fixed comparison point for the moisture trial. Finding the best fermentation time for my setup would require a separate time-course test. After incubation, I photographed the visible white growth and sent samples to Eurofins Singapore for Dumas protein analysis.

The pilot dataset has one protein result per fermented moisture level and one separately submitted unfermented baseline. I used the results to look for patterns and choose conditions to repeat. Independent repeats will show how much the response varies at each moisture level.

I used visible white coverage to describe how much of the surface had grown over by 48 hours. I also measured crude protein because a sample that shows more surface growth may still have a different proportion of protein in the whole fermented material. Comparing both outcomes helps me decide which moisture levels are worth repeating.

Preliminary results and interpretation

After 48 hours, the 50% sample still looked mostly granular, with little visible white growth. The 55% sample had patches of white growth with exposed okara between them. The 60% sample had a wider white layer with some okara still visible at the lower edge. At 65%, the white growth covered a large area and there were visible droplets. The 72.5% sample had a white layer in the centre with some exposed edges. The change between 55% and 60% was the main difference I wanted to check again. The packaging and camera angles differed across samples, so I used the photographs for a qualitative comparison of visible surface growth.

The photos show the samples at each starting moisture level. I cropped them from slide 12 of the interim presentation to focus on the samples.

I estimated dry-mass protein by assuming that the moisture at analysis equalled the starting moisture target and that the reported protein values were on an as-received basis.

At the 72.5% starting moisture target, the estimated crude protein concentration was 36.7% of dry mass for the fermented sample and 25.7% for the unfermented baseline. This is a difference of about 11.1 percentage points before rounding, or about 43% relative to the baseline. The comparison gives an initial indication of how crude protein concentration differed between the two samples. A repeat using a baseline from the same batch would make the comparison more reliable.

I plan to prepare and incubate the 55% and 60% conditions side by side to check whether the same difference in visible growth appears again.

I calculated the estimated dry-mass percentage by dividing the reported protein in grams per 100 g by the dry-matter fraction implied by the starting moisture target. For example, the 60% moisture target gives a dry-matter fraction of 0.40. Dividing the reported 11.0 g per 100 g by 0.40 gives 27.5 g per 100 g of dry mass. The estimates were close to 28% for the 50%, 55% and 60% treatments and higher for the 65% and 72.5% treatments.

A higher percentage of crude protein can also occur if the fungus uses up other dry components during fermentation. To check whether the total amount of crude protein changes, I would need to track the mass and dry-matter content before and after fermentation.

The protein results and photos therefore point to different parts of the next experiment. The photos suggest that 55% and 60% are useful levels to compare closely for visible growth. The estimated protein values show a different pattern across the five moisture treatments. I want to check whether these patterns repeat when I use the same batch for the treatments and collect the photographs under the same conditions.

Next experiments and deliverables

I plan to take photos at the same time, distance, angle and lighting for each sample. I will use the same approach to score visible surface coverage. If the difference between 55% and 60% appears again under the same conditions, I can add a moisture level between them. If it varies between runs, I can use the repeats to see how consistent the response is before choosing a moisture range.

For protein analysis, I will check how Eurofins reports the results. For this pilot, I estimated dry-mass protein using the starting moisture targets. In the next experiment, I plan to measure final moisture because the water fraction can change during fermentation and sample preparation. If I have enough sample and access to the oven and balance, I will mix the material and dry a small, representative portion in the same state as the submitted sample. I plan to include an unfermented sample from the same batch where feasible. The main comparison will be crude protein concentration. If I can also record total mass and dry-matter content before and after fermentation, I can estimate total crude protein recovery.

For the next set of samples, I plan to use the same preparation and submission steps for each moisture level. I will keep a record of the time between the end of fermentation and sample preparation, since this is the point where I want to compare the treatments. Recording these details will make it easier to link each laboratory result back to the correct treatment and to compare results across repeats.

Conclusion

I chose okara because it retains protein and fibre and gave me a clearly defined starting material. Earlier studies and the availability of a tempeh starter helped me choose R. oligosporus. After speaking with industry contacts, I decided to test how reducing moisture from the 72.5% reference affected visible growth and crude protein. The pilot photos showed a change between 55% and 60%, which I plan to check in repeat trials. The fermented sample at the 72.5% target also had a higher estimated dry-mass crude protein concentration than the unfermented baseline. Repeating the trial with measured final moisture and a same-batch baseline would help me check that comparison and decide which moisture levels to test across batches.
